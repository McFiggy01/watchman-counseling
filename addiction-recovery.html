Recovery Doesn't Have to Look Like One Thing

Addiction is complicated. It's not a willpower problem or a moral failure — it's a pattern that develops for reasons, often ones that made sense at the time. Harm reduction meets you where you actually are, without demanding you fit a particular mold before you deserve help. No shame. No one-size-fits-all. Just honest work toward something better.

What Brings People Here

◆

Struggling to Cut Back or Stop

You've tried to slow down or quit, but it keeps pulling you back. Wanting to stop and not being able to is one of the most frustrating parts of addiction — and it's not a character flaw.

◆

Use That's Affecting Real Life

Work, relationships, health, finances — when substance use starts costing you things you care about, that's usually the sign it's time to talk to someone.

◆

Coming Out of a Program

Finishing inpatient or intensive outpatient treatment is a beginning, not an ending. Ongoing outpatient therapy gives you continuity and support during the transition back to daily life.

◆

Understanding the Patterns Underneath

Substance use usually serves a function — managing stress, numbing pain, filling a gap. Therapy helps you understand what that function is and what else can serve it.

◆

Supporting a Loved One in Recovery

When someone you love is struggling with addiction, it affects you too. Family members and partners of people in recovery have their own needs — and those deserve attention.

◆

A Non-Judgmental Space to Be Honest

Sometimes you just need somewhere to say the full truth without bracing for a reaction. That's what therapy is — especially when the topic carries as much stigma as this one does.

The approach

How We Work With Addiction & Recovery

Addiction counseling at Watchman Counseling is grounded in a harm reduction model. That means the goal is determined by you — not by what a program thinks recovery should look like. For some people, the goal is abstinence. For others, it's reducing use, managing risk, or stabilizing a pattern that's gotten out of control. All of those are valid starting points.

Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW is OASAS-trained (New York State Office of Addiction Services and Supports), with real clinical experience in substance use settings — including work in the Bronx where the conversation about addiction was often urgent and multilayered. That background shapes a clinical approach that's grounded, direct, and free of the shame that too often gets layered onto this work.

We use Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) adapted for addiction — identifying triggers, understanding the thoughts and emotions that drive use, and building concrete alternatives. We look at what the substance has been doing for you, not just at the problem it's become.

If medication-assisted treatment (MAT) is part of the picture, we work collaboratively around that. If you're early in figuring out what you even want, that's also a fine place to start.

Common questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to be sober to start therapy? +
No. You don't need to be sober, abstinent, or at any particular stage of readiness to begin. Harm reduction starts where you are. If you're still using and want to talk about it honestly — that's exactly the kind of conversation therapy is for. You don't have to have it figured out before you show up.
What is harm reduction? +
Harm reduction is a framework that prioritizes safety and dignity over abstinence as a prerequisite for care. Rather than demanding someone be sober before they can get help, harm reduction meets people where they are and focuses on reducing the negative consequences of use — while moving toward the person's own goals at their own pace. It's not anti-recovery; it's pro-human.
Do you work with people on MAT (medication-assisted treatment)? +
Yes. Medication-assisted treatment — including buprenorphine (Suboxone), methadone, and naltrexone — is evidence-based and effective for opioid and alcohol use disorders. Therapy works alongside MAT, not against it. If you're on MAT or considering it, that's part of the clinical picture we work with, not a barrier to therapy.
