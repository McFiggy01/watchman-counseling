5 CBT Tools to Quiet Anxious Thinking

Practical, evidence-based techniques from Cognitive Behavioral Therapy — built for people who want to understand what's driving their anxiety and start responding to it differently.

Written by Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW · NY Licensed Clinical Social Worker · watchmancounseling.com

Anxiety is not a character flaw or a sign that something is fundamentally wrong with you. It's your nervous system trying to protect you — and sometimes overreacting. CBT gives you practical tools to interrupt that cycle and respond differently. These five techniques work. Use them consistently.
1
The Thought Record

Anxious thoughts feel like facts. They're not. A thought record helps you slow down and examine what's actually happening versus what your brain is predicting. Write down the situation, the automatic thought ("I'm going to fail"), the emotion and intensity (anxiety, 80%), and then ask: what's the evidence for and against this thought? What would I tell a friend who thought this?

Try it
Next time you feel anxious, grab a notebook. Write: (1) What just happened? (2) What thought came up? (3) What emotion, 0–100? (4) What's the realistic take? Rate the anxiety again after.
2
Scheduled Worry Time

Trying to stop worrying never works — it just moves the worry underground. Instead, give worry a designated slot: 15–20 minutes at the same time each day (not before bed). When a worry pops up outside that window, write it down and tell yourself: "I'll deal with that at 5pm." This trains your brain that worries have a container — they're not emergencies.

Try it
Pick a specific time and place for your daily worry window. Keep a notepad nearby to capture worries as they come up. When the time arrives, work through the list — then close it.
3
The 5-4-3-2-1 Grounding Technique

When anxiety spikes, your nervous system is in threat mode and your thinking goes offline. Grounding pulls you back into the present through your senses. Name: 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste. This isn't just distraction — it interrupts the physiological anxiety spiral by forcing your brain into sensory attention.

Try it
Practice this when you're not anxious first, so it becomes automatic. Run through it twice. Then use it the next time you feel anxiety rising before it peaks.
4
Evidence Testing

Anxious thinking tends to catastrophize — assuming the worst outcome and treating it as inevitable. Evidence testing asks you to put the belief on trial. Take the anxious thought and ask: What's the actual evidence this is true? Have I predicted this before and been wrong? What's the most realistic outcome, not the worst? What's one thing I could do if the feared thing actually happened?

Try it
Write your anxious thought as a statement ("I'm going to embarrass myself"). Then write three pieces of real evidence for it and three against it. What does the balance suggest?
5
Gradual Exposure (Do the Thing Anyway)

Avoidance is anxiety's best friend. Every time you avoid something that triggers anxiety, you tell your brain: "That was a real threat — good call." Gradual exposure works in reverse: you approach the feared situation in small, manageable steps until your nervous system learns it can handle it. Start with something that's a 3/10 on the fear scale, not a 10/10.

Try it
List one situation you've been avoiding. Break it into five smaller steps from easiest to hardest. Do step one this week. Notice that anxiety rises, peaks, and then drops — that drop is your nervous system updating its threat model.
