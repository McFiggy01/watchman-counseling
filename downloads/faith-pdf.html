When Faith and Struggle Meet: A Christian's Guide to Seeking Help

Answers to the questions many believers are afraid to ask — about depression, anxiety, therapy, and what it means to need help when you have faith.

Written by Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW · NY Licensed Clinical Social Worker · watchmancounseling.com

Faith is real. Mental health struggles are also real. These two truths are not in conflict — but the church has not always made that clear. If you've been told to "just pray more," or felt like struggling meant your faith was weak, this guide is for you. You don't have to choose between your beliefs and your healing.
1
Struggling Is Not a Spiritual Failure

Scripture is full of people in profound emotional distress — David crying out in the Psalms, Elijah asking God to take his life, Paul describing a "thorn in the flesh" that wasn't removed despite prayer. Mental health struggles are part of the human condition, not evidence of weak faith. Depression has neurobiological roots. Anxiety has neurological underpinnings. These are not spiritual deficits — they are conditions that affect real people, including deeply faithful ones.

Reflect on this
Where did you first learn that struggling meant spiritual weakness? Was that message from Scripture — or from people interpreting it? What does the actual text show you about how God responds to those in pain?
2
Therapy and Faith Are Not Competing

Seeking a doctor for a physical illness is not a failure of faith — it's wisdom. Seeking a therapist for a mental health struggle is the same. God heals in many ways. Therapy is a tool, like medicine, that works with the capacity God built into the human mind and relationship to bring healing. Faith-integrated therapy specifically works to hold both — your clinical needs and your spiritual identity — in the same space. You don't have to leave your faith at the door.

Reflect on this
Have you been waiting for God to fix something before you act? What would it look like to view therapy as a form of stewardship — taking responsibility for the life and mind you've been given?
3
Give Yourself Permission to Be Honest With God

Many believers perform for God — presenting the "right" emotions in prayer, suppressing grief or anger because it doesn't feel "faith-filled." But the Psalms — which make up the largest book in the Bible — are saturated with lament, anger, despair, and question. God already knows your interior world. Bringing it honestly into your prayer life is not a lack of faith; it's the truest form of it. Pretending to feel what you don't is spiritual performance, not relationship.

Try it
Read Psalm 22 or Psalm 88. Notice what David or the psalmist actually says to God — the raw, unfiltered state. Write your own honest version. No performance required.
4
Process Church Hurt Without Losing Your Faith

Church hurt is real — spiritual abuse, religious shaming, manipulation under the banner of faith leaves deep wounds that often show up in the therapy office as anxiety, depression, and complex grief. The key distinction: the wound came from people and institutions, not from God. Separating the two — processing the specific harm caused by specific people — allows faith to survive and heal even when the church community did damage. This is work therapy can hold.

Reflect on this
Where has the pain come specifically from? Who or what institution caused it? What parts of your faith feel genuinely yours, separate from the people who hurt you? Those parts are worth protecting.
5
Use Spiritual Practices as Clinical Tools

Prayer, meditation on Scripture, worship, and community are not just spiritual disciplines — they have documented effects on the nervous system, emotional regulation, and sense of meaning. When these practices feel empty or impossible during a mental health crisis, that is often a symptom, not a sign of lost faith. Integrating faith practices with clinical tools — using Scripture alongside CBT, grounding alongside prayer — produces outcomes that neither approach achieves alone.

Try it
Identify one spiritual practice that has historically brought you peace. Pair it with a physical regulation practice (breathing, grounding, movement). Notice how the two reinforce each other. This integration is what faith-integrated therapy makes possible.
