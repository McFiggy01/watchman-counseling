Understanding Your Nervous System After Trauma

What trauma actually does to your brain and body — and five foundational practices for helping your nervous system find safety again.

Written by Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW · NY Licensed Clinical Social Worker · watchmancounseling.com

Trauma is not weakness. It is a normal nervous system response to abnormal experiences. Your reactions — the hypervigilance, the startle response, the emotional flooding, the numbness — are not signs that something is wrong with you. They are signs that your nervous system did exactly what it was designed to do. Healing means teaching it that the threat is over.
1
Learn Your Window of Tolerance

The Window of Tolerance is the zone where you can function, process, and engage — not too activated (hyperarousal: panic, rage, flashback), not too shut down (hypoarousal: numbness, dissociation, collapse). After trauma, this window often narrows dramatically. Almost anything can push you outside it. Healing expands the window over time. Knowing where you are right now — hyper, hypo, or in the window — is the starting point for every other regulation skill.

Try it
At three points during your day, check in: Am I activated (racing heart, edge, hypervigilance)? Or shut down (foggy, numb, disconnected)? Or in the window (present, functional, able to respond)? Just naming it without judgment is a regulation skill.
2
Use Physiology to Downregulate

When your nervous system is in hyperarousal — flooded with cortisol, heart racing, body braced — talking and thinking are limited tools. The quickest path down is through the body. Extended exhales (longer out than in) activate the parasympathetic nervous system directly. The physiological sigh — a double inhale through the nose followed by a long exhale — is the fastest known reset. Cold water on the face and wrists also works through the dive reflex.

Try it
Practice box breathing daily — not only in crisis: inhale 4 counts, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4. Practice when calm so it becomes automatic when you're not. The nervous system learns through repetition, not just intention.
3
Build a Safe-Place Anchor

A safe-place anchor is a detailed mental image of a real or imagined place where you felt (or can imagine feeling) completely safe, calm, and present. When activated well, it engages the same neurological pathways as actual physical safety. This is not escapism — it's a regulated, practiced intervention used in trauma-focused therapy. The key is building the image in sensory detail when you're calm, so it's accessible when you're not.

Try it
Close your eyes. Picture a place where you feel genuinely safe and at peace. Build it in detail: What do you see? What do you hear? What does the air feel like? What's the temperature? Practice visiting it for 5 minutes daily until it becomes an automatic resource.
4
Work With Triggers, Not Against Them

A trigger is not an overreaction. It is your nervous system accurately detecting a sensory cue (smell, sound, visual, posture, time of year) that it has associated with past danger. The goal is not to eliminate triggers — it's to widen the gap between trigger and response. That gap is where choice lives. The first step is mapping your triggers without judgment: identifying the sensory inputs that reliably activate your nervous system.

Try it
After a strong activation, work backwards: what were you seeing, hearing, smelling, or feeling physically right before it hit? Build a list over time. Not to avoid triggers permanently — but to know what you're working with and prepare your response.
5
Co-Regulation: Safety Is Found in Relationship

Human nervous systems co-regulate — we borrow calm from other calm people. Trauma often damages the capacity for safe relationship, making connection feel dangerous at the same time the nervous system desperately needs it. Working gradually toward safe connection — with one trusted person, a therapist, or even a calm animal — is not supplemental to trauma recovery. It is central to it. The nervous system heals in the context of safe relationship more than in any other context.

Try it
Identify one relationship that feels even partially safe. Notice how your body feels in that person's presence versus in activation. That difference — your nervous system calming with another person — is co-regulation working. Seek more of it deliberately.
