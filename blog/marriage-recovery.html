When One Person in a Marriage Is in Recovery: What Therapists See That Couples Don't

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you or your partner is struggling with addiction or relationship issues, please reach out to a licensed professional.

One of the most common misconceptions I encounter in my work is this: if the person with the addiction gets sober, the marriage will naturally heal on its own.

I understand why people believe that. The addiction was the visible problem. The addiction caused most of the damage. So if the addiction stops, the damage stops, and the healing begins.

In theory, that makes sense. In practice, it is rarely that simple.

What I see in my office, over and over, is couples who made it through the addiction and got to the other side of sobriety and then ran into something they didn't expect: the relationship was still broken. In some ways, the relationship was more complicated now that the addiction was gone. They had survived the storm, but they didn't know how to live in the quiet.

The Marriage Develops Its Own Patterns Around the Addiction

Addiction doesn't just affect the person who has it. It reshapes everything around it, including the marriage.

Over months and years, a couple living with addiction develops an entire system of coping. Who covers for whom. Who manages the crises. Who keeps the kids from knowing what's happening. Who holds the family together when the other person can't. Who walks on eggshells and when. Who apologizes. Who forgives. How conflict gets avoided, or how it erupts and then gets smoothed over until the next time.

These patterns become the marriage. The couple often can't see them clearly because they're inside them. But when addiction ends and sobriety begins, those patterns don't automatically disappear. The systems that were built to manage the addiction stay in place even when the addiction is gone. And they create their own problems.

What the Non-Addicted Partner Is Actually Going Through

I want to spend some time here because this part is often overlooked.

The partner who was not using often carries wounds that are just as significant as their spouse's, and they carry them with less support. Nobody is holding meetings for them. The cultural attention goes to the person in recovery. The partner who held everything together while things were falling apart often doesn't have a name for what they've been through.

What I see in that partner, clinically, often looks like this:

  • Hypervigilance. They have been monitoring their spouse's behavior for so long that they can't stop. Every late night, every mood shift, every unusual phone call triggers an alarm response they can't fully control.
  • Grief. They are grieving the years they lost. The version of their spouse they imagined they were marrying. The relationship they thought they would have. That grief doesn't disappear when sobriety begins. It often surfaces more clearly once the crisis is over.
  • Resentment they feel guilty about. They know they're supposed to be supportive. They know their partner is working hard. But they are also exhausted and angry, and they don't know where to put that.
  • A loss of identity. They built their entire functioning around managing a household in crisis. Now that the crisis has changed, they're not sure who they are or what role they play.

That partner needs support too. Not as an afterthought. As a priority.

The Unexpected Turbulence of Early Sobriety

Here is something that surprises many couples: the early months of sobriety are often harder on the marriage than the addiction was.

This sounds backward, but it makes clinical sense. During the addiction, both partners were in survival mode. There was always a crisis to manage, a fire to put out. The emotional reality of the relationship got pushed aside because there was always something more urgent to deal with.

When the addiction stops, the urgency stops. And into that quiet come all the things that got buried: the unspoken resentments, the intimacy that stopped, the trust that was broken and never fully repaired, the grief, the anger, the fear. None of those things went away. They were just waiting.

The partner in recovery is often newly raw. The emotional numbing that addiction provided is gone. They may be encountering their own feelings in a way they haven't in years. They may be struggling with shame, with the weight of what they've done, with who they were during the addiction.

Meanwhile, the non-addicted partner may have been hoping that sobriety would finally allow them to relax, and instead they are still managing and still worried and still not getting what they need. The relationship tension can actually increase in this period, not decrease.

The Role Shift That Happens When Recovery Begins

For a long time, one partner held most of the power in the relationship by necessity. They made the decisions. They managed the finances and the children and the household. They were the stable one.

When their spouse enters recovery and begins to show up differently, it creates a genuine disruption in the structure of the relationship. The recovering partner wants to take back responsibilities. They want to be trusted. They want to be seen as capable again. That is entirely reasonable and it is part of healthy recovery.

But the partner who held those responsibilities for years may not be ready to hand them back. They may not trust yet. They may have learned, the hard way, that they can only count on themselves, and that lesson doesn't erase easily. They may hold on to control not out of malice but out of self-protection.

From the outside, this can look like the non-addicted partner refusing to let their spouse change. What it actually is, more often, is someone whose nervous system learned a lesson over years of broken promises, and who needs time and evidence to unlearn it.

Both people are reasonable. Both people are hurting. And they are often hurting in ways that work directly against each other, which is why a therapist in the room matters.

What Therapists See That Couples Can't

When I work with couples navigating recovery, there are several things I watch for that are almost impossible to see from inside the relationship.

The identified patient problem. The couple has organized their entire understanding of "what's wrong" around one person. The addict was the problem. Now the addict is in recovery, so the problem should be solved. But relationships don't work that way. The dynamic between two people has its own history, its own wounds, its own patterns. Treating only one person's individual recovery rarely heals the relationship. The relationship itself needs attention.

Codependency that outlasted the addiction. Many partners of people in recovery developed their own patterns of over-functioning, people-pleasing, and suppressing their own needs in order to manage the relationship. Those patterns are not automatically healthy just because the person they developed around is now sober. They often need to be examined and reworked in their own right.

Trust being rebuilt at mismatched speeds. The recovering partner often wants trust restored faster than it can reasonably be rebuilt. The non-addicted partner often rebuilds trust more slowly than either of them would like. Neither pace is wrong. But when the mismatch isn't named and worked through, it becomes its own source of conflict. He thinks she doesn't believe he's changed. She thinks he doesn't understand what she went through. Both of them are right, and neither of them can hear the other clearly without help.

Intimacy that went dormant and doesn't automatically return. Emotional and physical intimacy in a marriage with active addiction often shuts down as a survival mechanism. Once the addiction ends, many couples expect intimacy to resume naturally. It rarely does without intentional work. There is too much history in the way.

What Actually Helps

I want to offer something practical, not just a clinical description of what goes wrong.

First: individual therapy for both partners, not just the one in recovery. The non-addicted partner has been through something significant. They deserve their own space to process it, with their own therapist, not just as a supporting actor in their spouse's recovery story.

Second: couples therapy, ideally with someone who has experience in addiction and recovery. This is not optional. A marriage that survived addiction and is now trying to rebuild is not the same as a marriage working through ordinary conflict. The dynamics are specific and they require specific knowledge to navigate.

Third: patience with the timeline, but not passivity about the work. Recovery takes time. The marriage recovering takes time. But time alone doesn't heal it. Time plus intentional work does. Couples who sit back and wait for things to get better on their own often find, a year later, that they are still in the same stuck place.

Fourth: naming what both people have been through. The conversation I try to create in the room is one where both partners feel seen, not just the one who had the addiction. The partner who held the family together for years while carrying their own pain needs to hear, out loud, that what they did was hard and that it cost them something. That acknowledgment matters more than most people realize.

A Note to the Couple That Is in This Right Now

If you are in recovery and your marriage is struggling, that does not mean you failed. It does not mean your sobriety isn't real. It means you are two people trying to rebuild something that took significant damage, and rebuilding takes longer and requires more work than most people are prepared for.

If you are the partner of someone in recovery and you are struggling, that does not mean you are unsupportive or that you don't love your spouse. It means you have been carrying a very heavy load for a very long time, and you deserve to set some of it down in a safe place with someone who can help you sort through it.

The goal of couples therapy in this context is not to go back to what the marriage was before. In most cases, what it was before wasn't working, or the addiction wouldn't have taken root the way it did. The goal is to build something better. Something honest. Something that both people actually chose.

That is possible. I have seen it happen. But it doesn't happen on its own.
