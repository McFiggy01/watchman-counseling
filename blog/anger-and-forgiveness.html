Anger and Forgiveness: Letting Go Without Excusing What Happened

Why holding onto resentment costs you more than it costs the person who hurt you, and how to move forward without pretending it was okay.

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW  ·  Part 2 of 4

Understanding Anger: From Wounds to Healing — Part 2 of 4. Last week in Part 1 we explored how unresolved trauma shows up as anger. This week: the hardest conversation in recovery and healing — forgiveness.
Note: This article is for educational purposes only and does not replace individualized mental health assessment, diagnosis, or treatment. If you are struggling with anger, resentment, or unresolved trauma, please consider reaching out to a licensed therapist.

Someone hurt you. Maybe they hurt you badly. Maybe they never apologized. Maybe they do not even remember what they did.

And now someone is telling you to forgive them.

If that sentence made you tense, you are not alone. Forgiveness is one of the most misunderstood concepts in both mental health and faith communities. It gets presented as something you owe the person who wronged you, as a sign of weakness, or as a requirement to reconcile with someone who was harmful.

None of that is true.

Forgiveness does not mean what happened was acceptable. It does not mean you are required to trust that person again. It does not mean you stop feeling hurt. And it does not mean the relationship continues as if nothing occurred.

What forgiveness actually means, and why it matters for your healing, is worth understanding clearly.

What Forgiveness Is Not

Before we talk about what forgiveness is, it helps to name what it is not. Many people resist forgiving because they have been handed a distorted version of it.

Forgiveness is not forgetting. Healthy forgiveness does not require you to pretend that harmful things did not happen. Memory serves a protective function. Remembering what someone did can help you make wise decisions about how much access you give them to your life going forward.

Forgiveness is not excusing the behavior. Saying "what you did was wrong and caused real harm" and saying "I choose to release my resentment toward you" are not mutually exclusive. You can hold both at the same time.

Forgiveness is not reconciliation. Reconciliation requires two willing people, genuine accountability, and rebuilt trust over time. Forgiveness requires only one person: you. You can forgive someone and still choose not to have them in your life. You can forgive an abusive parent and still maintain distance. You can forgive a former partner and still hold a protective boundary.

Forgiveness is not a one-time event. For many people, forgiveness is a process. You may choose to forgive, feel the peace of that decision, and then wake up three days later feeling the anger return. That does not mean you failed. It means forgiveness, like grief, moves in waves. The choosing matters more than the feeling.

What Forgiveness Actually Is

Everett Worthington, a psychologist who has researched forgiveness for decades, describes it as a process of replacing negative emotions toward someone, such as bitterness, resentment, and hostility, with more neutral or even positive ones.

The key word in that definition is replacement. Forgiveness is not the removal of all feeling. It is a conscious movement away from emotions that keep you tethered to the injury.

A simpler way to think about it: resentment is like drinking poison and waiting for the other person to get sick.

When you hold onto bitterness toward someone who hurt you, the resentment lives in your body, not theirs. It shapes how you sleep, how you relate to people around you, how you experience trust, and how much emotional energy you have for the things you love. The person who caused the harm often goes about their life completely unaffected by your anger. Meanwhile, you carry it everywhere.

Forgiveness is the decision to set down that weight. Not for them. For you.

The Physical and Emotional Cost of Resentment

Chronic resentment is not just an emotional experience. Research from the Mayo Clinic and others in the field of mind-body medicine has linked unresolved anger and resentment to elevated cortisol, increased cardiovascular risk, disrupted sleep, and weakened immune function.

Beyond the physical, resentment tends to narrow your world over time. People who carry deep bitterness often find that it begins to color everything. A new relationship feels dangerous because of what the old one did. A reasonable disagreement at work triggers the same feeling as an old wound at home. Hope feels reckless because hurt has felt inevitable.

Resentment has a way of becoming part of your identity before you realize it happened.

This is particularly relevant for people working through trauma. As we discussed in Part 1, unresolved emotional pain often shows up as anger in the present. Resentment is what happens when that anger has no place to go. It hardens. It becomes a wall. And while the wall keeps people out, it also keeps you in.

Can You Forgive Someone Who Never Apologized?

This is the question most people are really asking when they resist the idea of forgiveness.

The short answer is yes. And sometimes, it is the only path forward.

Not everyone who hurt you will acknowledge what they did. Some people lack the self-awareness. Some are no longer alive. Some are too defended by shame or addiction to face the impact of their choices. Waiting for an apology that may never come means placing your healing in someone else's hands.

That is too much power to give to a person who has already caused you harm.

Forgiveness without an apology does not mean the other person gets away with anything. It means you have decided that your peace is not contingent on their accountability. Those are separate things. Accountability belongs to them. Your healing belongs to you.

When Anger Feels Protective

One reason people hold onto resentment even when it causes suffering is that anger can feel like a shield. As long as you are angry, you feel in control. Letting go of the anger can feel like letting your guard down, or even like losing something that proves what happened to you was real.

This is worth naming, because it is real. For some people, the anger is the only thing that has ever validated their experience. If no one acknowledged what happened, the anger becomes the evidence. Releasing it can feel like erasing the injury.

But your anger does not have to be the keeper of your story. What happened to you was real. It was wrong. It had consequences. Those truths do not disappear when you choose to stop letting resentment run your life.

You are allowed to say: "What happened was real and it was wrong, and I am no longer willing to let it cost me the rest of my life."

Five Steps Toward Genuine Forgiveness

Forgiveness is not a feeling you summon. It is a direction you choose and keep choosing.

1. Acknowledge the full weight of the harm

Cheap forgiveness glosses over the injury. Real forgiveness begins by being honest about what actually happened and how it affected you. This is not about dwelling in the wound permanently. It is about giving yourself permission to name it fully, perhaps in a journal, in therapy, or in prayer, before you move toward release.

You cannot let go of something you have never fully picked up and examined.

2. Separate the person from the act

Forgiving someone does not require you to see them as a good person or minimize what they did. It does require recognizing that human beings are more than the worst things they have done, including you. This is not a step toward excusing the harm. It is a step toward releasing yourself from the weight of hatred.

3. Make a decision, not just a feeling

Forgiveness rarely arrives as a warm emotional wave. More often, it begins as a deliberate choice made in the absence of any particular feeling. You decide to forgive not because you feel like it, but because you understand that continuing to carry the resentment is costing you something important.

Write it down if it helps. Say it out loud. Bring it to God in prayer. The decision is real even when the feeling has not fully caught up.

4. Set appropriate boundaries

Forgiving someone does not mean returning to a situation that was harmful. Part of healing is learning to protect yourself wisely. You can extend forgiveness to someone and still choose not to share your time, your home, your vulnerability, or your trust with them again. Boundaries are not the opposite of forgiveness. They are often what makes forgiveness sustainable.

5. Revisit the decision when the anger returns

As mentioned earlier, forgiveness is often a process rather than a single moment. When resentment surfaces again, as it frequently does, return to the decision you made. Not with shame, but with patience. The willingness to keep choosing forgiveness, even when it is difficult, is itself a form of healing.

A Spiritual Reflection: The Weight of an Unpaid Debt

In Matthew 18, Jesus tells a story about a servant who owed an enormous debt and was forgiven by his master. That same servant then went out and refused to forgive a much smaller debt owed to him.

The parable is not primarily about keeping score. It is about the posture of a person who has received mercy. The servant's refusal to extend what he had received revealed something about his heart.

For those who have experienced God's forgiveness, the call to forgive others is not a burden layered on top of an already hard life. It is an invitation into the freedom that forgiveness actually creates.

Ephesians 4:31-32 puts it plainly: "Let all bitterness, wrath, anger, clamor, and evil speaking be put away from you, with all malice. And be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God in Christ forgave you."

This is not a command to minimize what happened to you. It is a call to refuse letting bitterness have the last word in your story.

If the idea of forgiving someone who hurt you feels impossible right now, that is an honest place to be. You do not have to be fully ready. You simply have to be willing to become willing. That willingness, brought honestly before God, is often where the process begins.

Forgiveness Is Not the Finish Line

Something important to say before closing: forgiveness is not the end of the work. It is a significant turning point, but the anger that led to this conversation often has roots that go deeper than any single injury.

If you have found yourself nodding along to both parts of this series, that is not a coincidence. Trauma, resentment, and the habits of anger that develop around them are connected. Addressing one without the others tends to produce incomplete healing.

The next two articles will go deeper into the practical skills of managing anger in real time, and how to handle anger inside close relationships without destroying the connection.

But for today, consider this question:

"Is there someone in your life, or in your past, whose debt you are still holding? And what is carrying it actually costing you?"

You are not required to answer that question today. But it is worth sitting with.
