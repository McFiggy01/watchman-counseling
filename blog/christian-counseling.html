Christian Counseling and Mental Health

How therapy can help with depression, anxiety, trauma, and anger while making room for your faith.

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you're experiencing significant distress, please reach out to a licensed therapist.

You can pray every morning and still struggle to get out of bed. You can trust God and still feel anxious when the phone rings. And you can love your family and still need help with how you respond when you're angry. These are struggles you can talk about openly in counseling.

What is Christian counseling?

In a mental health setting, Christian counseling can mean working with a licensed therapist who makes room for your Christian beliefs as part of your care. You might want to talk about how your faith helps you cope, how a painful experience has affected your relationship with God, or how your values can guide a difficult decision. If you want to include prayer or Scripture, you and your therapist can discuss when that would be helpful. You can also keep sessions focused on practical coping skills and bring up faith whenever it feels relevant.

Researchers reviewed 97 studies on therapy that incorporated clients' religious or spiritual beliefs. In the more rigorous comparisons, faith-adapted therapy and standard therapy showed similar improvements in emotional distress. Clients who received faith-adapted therapy reported greater gains in spiritual well-being. If faith is important to you, that's one reason you might want to bring it into the conversation. What might counseling look like?

Depression. Depression can show up as low energy, changes in sleep, or losing interest in things you used to enjoy. Even returning a friend's call can feel like too much. A therapist might help you choose one manageable step, like sending that friend a text, and explore thoughts like "I'm a burden." If your faith matters here, you can also talk honestly about what it feels like to pray when God seems far away. NIMH guide to depression

Anxiety. Imagine your boss texts, "Can we talk tomorrow?" and you spend the evening convinced you're about to lose your job. When worry is hard to control, it can interfere with sleep, concentration, and daily life. In therapy, you might practice separating what you know from what you fear and taking one practical step while you wait for an answer. If prayer is part of your life, you might use it to express your fears honestly as you practice handling uncertainty. NIMH guide to anxiety

Trauma. After a frightening or harmful experience, you might have nightmares, feel on edge, or avoid places that remind you of what happened. A therapist trained in trauma can explain treatment options and work with you on how to begin. If the experience happened in a church or involved someone you trusted spiritually, that belongs in the conversation too. You get to decide whether you want spiritual practices included in your care. NIMH guide to trauma and PTSD

Anger management. Anger is a normal emotion. Counseling can help when your reactions hurt people or leave you regretting what you said or did. You might learn to notice an early warning sign, like a tight jaw or a rising voice, and say, "I need ten minutes to cool down. I'll come back so we can finish talking." If patience and self-control are part of how you want to live out your faith, this gives you a concrete way to practice them. SAMHSA anger management guide

How to take the first step

If these struggles are affecting your sleep, work, relationships, or daily routines, consider talking with a licensed mental health professional. In a first consultation, you can ask, "How do you work with what I'm going through, and how would you include my Christian faith if I wanted that?" Listen for a clear answer about both the therapy they offer and how they'll respect your preferences.

You can start with one honest sentence. "I've been carrying this for a while, and I'd like some help."
