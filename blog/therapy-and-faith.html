Does Therapy Conflict With Faith? What to Tell Christians Who Won't Go

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you're experiencing significant distress, please reach out to a licensed therapist.

I have had the same conversation dozens of times in my career.

Someone finally agrees to come in. They sit down. We talk for a while. At some point they say, sometimes directly and sometimes sideways, something like this:

"I wasn't sure I should be here. My pastor said I should pray more. My family thinks I just need more faith. I feel a little guilty being here instead of just trusting God."

I understand where that comes from.

I also think it has cost a lot of Christians a great deal of unnecessary suffering.

So let me address it directly, not as a theologian, but as a licensed clinician and a man of faith who has sat in both the therapist's chair and the pew.

Where the Suspicion Comes From

The skepticism isn't baseless.

Psychology did not emerge from the church. Freud was openly hostile to religion and said so plainly. Much of twentieth-century psychological theory assumed that religious belief was a kind of coping mechanism, a projection or neurosis, something to be worked through rather than honored.

That history is real.

So is the more recent concern: many Christians worry that a secular therapist will gently challenge their beliefs, reframe their values, nudge them toward conclusions the church would not sanction. They have heard stories, sometimes true ones, of therapists who treated faith as the problem rather than the resource.

Those concerns are worth taking seriously.

But they describe a particular kind of therapist, not therapy itself.

What Therapy Actually Is

Therapy, at its core, is a structured conversation aimed at understanding patterns of thought, emotion, behavior, and relationship that are causing suffering or limiting a person's life.

It is not a worldview transplant.

Good therapy does not tell you what to believe. It helps you understand why you feel what you feel, why you do what you do, and what you might do differently. The values that guide those choices remain yours.

A skilled therapist working with a person of faith does not ask them to set their faith aside. They ask: what does your faith say about this? How does your relationship with God connect to what you're experiencing? Where does your theology give you comfort, and where does it add weight?

That is not a threat to faith.

That is faith being taken seriously.

The Body-Soul Problem Christians Often Overlook

If a Christian breaks their leg, nobody tells them to pray harder and skip the emergency room.

If a Christian is diagnosed with diabetes, nobody suggests that insulin is a sign of weak faith.

If a Christian develops heart disease, we do not tell them to simply trust God and decline the cardiologist.

We understand that the body is biological. It gets sick, it needs treatment, and seeking medical care is responsible stewardship of the life God gave us.

But the brain is also biological.

Depression is not a spiritual failure. Anxiety is not a character defect. Trauma does not mean God has abandoned someone. These are real conditions with real neurological components, and they respond to real treatment.

The idea that we would treat every organ in the body medically except the brain, and call that faith, is not a biblical position. It is a cultural one, and it has caused real harm.

What Scripture Actually Says About Getting Help

Proverbs 11:14 says: "Where there is no guidance, a people falls, but in an abundance of counselors there is safety."

Proverbs 15:22: "Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed."

Proverbs 20:18: "Plans are established by counsel."

The wisdom tradition of Scripture does not tell people to figure everything out alone through prayer. It repeatedly commends the seeking of wise counsel as prudent, not faithless.

Galatians 6:2 says to "bear one another's burdens." That assumes burdens exist. It assumes we cannot always carry them alone. It assumes the community, not just the individual and God, is part of how healing happens.

There is nothing in Scripture that says a licensed professional trained in human psychology cannot be one of those counselors.

The Objection That Won't Go Away: "I Should Just Pray"

I believe in prayer.

I pray. I have prayed through some of the hardest chapters of my own life. I have watched prayer do things I cannot explain clinically.

And I will also tell you this:

In twenty-five years of clinical work, I have sat with hundreds of people who prayed faithfully, loved God genuinely, attended church regularly. And they were still drowning.

Not because their prayer was insufficient.

Not because their faith was weak.

But because prayer and therapy are not the same thing, and they are not in competition. They address different dimensions of a person's experience, and most people who are genuinely struggling need both.

Prayer connects you to God. Therapy helps you understand yourself. Neither one makes the other unnecessary.

A broken leg does not mean God isn't healing you. It means He may be healing you through the hands of a surgeon.

The Real Cost of the "Just Have Faith" Message

I want to be honest about what I have seen when this message goes unchallenged.

I have sat with people who spent years in depression, sometimes decades, because their church told them getting better was a matter of spiritual discipline. They prayed more. They read more Scripture. They confessed more. They kept showing up.

And they kept suffering.

And they began to believe the problem was with them, not with the advice they were given.

Some of them eventually developed the belief that they were beyond healing. That God had given up on them. That their suffering was evidence of some deep spiritual inadequacy they couldn't name.

That is a spiritual wound caused by bad theology about mental health.

I have also sat with people who finally came to therapy, sometimes after years of resistance, and within months began to experience what they described as a deeper relationship with God than they had ever had. Not despite the therapy. Because of what the therapy helped them understand about themselves.

What to Say to the Christian Who Still Won't Go

If you are trying to encourage someone you love to get help, here are a few things that tend to actually land:

Lead with the body. "You'd go to the doctor for anything else that was affecting you this much. Your brain is part of your body."

Ask about a Christian therapist specifically. The objection is often not to therapy itself but to the fear of values conflict. A therapist who shares their faith, or who explicitly works within it, changes the equation. Many of us exist.

Separate the symptom from the character. "Struggling doesn't mean you're failing God. It means you're human. Even Paul said he was given a thorn in the flesh he prayed to have removed. And it wasn't removed."

Name what you're actually seeing. Not "you seem depressed." Try something like: "I've watched you pull away from people you love. I've seen you stop doing the things that used to give you life. That's not a faith problem. That's something that needs attention."

Don't make it about convincing them. Offer once, clearly. Then let it rest. The person who is told five times feels pressured; the person who was told once and knew the door remained open comes back when they're ready.

A Word to the Person Reading This Who Is Still on the Fence

You may be the one who isn't sure whether going to therapy would be a faithful thing to do.

I want to say this plainly:

Seeking help is not a failure of faith. It is an act of stewardship: caring for the body and mind God gave you, the relationships that depend on you being well, and the work you were made to do.

You do not have to have it figured out before you come in. You do not have to be in crisis. You do not have to abandon what you believe in order to understand yourself better.

Faith and therapy are not enemies.

For a lot of people, they turn out to be companions.
