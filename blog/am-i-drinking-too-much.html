Am I Drinking Too Much? What to Ask Yourself Before the New Year

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment or medical advice. If you are concerned about your alcohol use, please speak with a licensed professional. Alcohol withdrawal can be medically serious — do not attempt to stop heavy daily drinking without medical supervision.

The holiday season is one of the most socially acceptable times to drink heavily. Office parties, family gatherings, New Year's Eve — there's a drink at every event and an invitation to have another. For most people, this is a temporary pattern that normalizes after January. But for some people, the holidays reveal something that was already there.

If you've found yourself wondering — genuinely wondering — whether you drink too much, that question is worth taking seriously. Not with shame, but with honesty.

The cultural noise around holiday drinking

Alcohol is uniquely normalized in ways other substances aren't. It's marketed as relaxation, celebration, sophistication, and social lubricant simultaneously. At the holidays, it's practically required — and drinking less than everyone else makes you the odd one out.

This cultural pressure makes it genuinely hard to see clearly. It's easy to mistake "everyone drinks like this in December" for "my drinking is fine." The two things are not the same. What's normal in a culture and what's healthy for an individual are different questions.

Signs your drinking may have crossed a line

Alcohol use disorder exists on a spectrum. You don't have to be drinking at breakfast or have lost your job to have a problem worth addressing. Here are some honest questions:

  • Do you drink more than you planned to? You tell yourself you'll have two drinks and then don't stop at two. Not occasionally, but regularly.
  • Do you drink to manage emotions? Stress, anxiety, loneliness, boredom, conflict — do you reach for alcohol as the primary way to handle these?
  • Has someone who cares about you expressed concern? Partners, family members, friends — if someone has mentioned your drinking and you dismissed it, that's worth revisiting.
  • Do you think about drinking more than you used to? Anticipating when you can drink, feeling restless if you can't, planning your schedule around it.
  • Do you drink to feel normal? Early in alcohol dependence, people drink to feel good. Later, they drink to feel like themselves — to relieve anxiety or irritability that emerged from not drinking.
  • Have you tried to cut back and found it hard? This is one of the clearest indicators. The difference between "I could stop if I wanted to" and actually stopping when you try to is diagnostic.
  • Are there consequences you've been rationalizing? Arguments, embarrassing moments, impaired mornings, things you said or did that you regret — if there's a pattern of consequences tied to drinking that you've been explaining away, pay attention to that.

The difference between celebrating and coping

There's a meaningful clinical distinction between drinking to enhance an experience and drinking to escape one. Social drinking at a party is different from drinking alone on a Tuesday because work was hard. Neither is automatically a problem, but the motivation matters.

When alcohol becomes a primary coping mechanism — when it's the main way you manage stress, numb pain, or escape from your life — that's a different relationship with drinking than casual social use. And that relationship has a trajectory. It tends to deepen over time, not resolve on its own.

Many people I work with in addiction recovery describe a window — sometimes years long — when they could feel that something was off, but told themselves it wasn't a problem yet. The question is whether you want to wait for the problem to become undeniable, or whether you're willing to look at it honestly now.

What to do if you recognize yourself in this

Start with honesty, not a verdict. You don't have to decide right now whether you're "an alcoholic." That label can be more of a barrier than a help. What you can do is get honest about the pattern and talk to someone qualified to help you assess it.

Talk to your doctor or a therapist. Alcohol use disorder is a clinical condition, not a moral failure, and it responds to treatment. Brief interventions, therapy, medication-assisted treatment, and support groups all have evidence behind them. You don't have to white-knuckle sobriety alone.

Don't try to detox on your own if you drink heavily every day. Alcohol withdrawal can be medically dangerous for people with physical dependence. If you drink heavily every day and want to stop, speak with a doctor before stopping abruptly.

Pay attention to what the drinking is covering. In my clinical experience, alcohol use at a problematic level is almost always managing something — anxiety, trauma, depression, unprocessed grief, relational pain. Treating the alcohol without addressing the underlying issue tends to produce relapse. Addressing both together is where real recovery happens.

The new year doesn't require a resolution — it invites honesty

New Year's resolutions fail at a high rate because they're usually built on willpower rather than understanding. "I'm going to drink less" collapses when the conditions that drive the drinking are still there.

What creates change is different: understanding why the pattern exists, finding support that addresses the root, and building a life where alcohol isn't needed in the same way. That's slower. It requires honesty and usually requires help. But it lasts.

There's a reason so many recovery narratives, across traditions, involve the language of freedom. John 8:36 says: "So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed." That freedom is not a resolution. It's a process — sometimes long and hard — that most people find they cannot walk alone.

If you're reading this and something in it landed, that's not an accident. The willingness to look honestly at this is itself a first step. Take it seriously.
