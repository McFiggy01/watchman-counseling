Grief During the Holidays: When Everyone Else Seems Fine and You're Not

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you're experiencing significant distress, please reach out to a licensed therapist.

There's a particular kind of loneliness that comes with grieving during the holidays. It's not just the absence of the person you lost. It's being surrounded by people who seem to be moving through the season normally while you feel like you're watching from the other side of a window. The world is decorated and celebrating, and you are carrying something they can't see.

If this is your first holiday season without someone, it may be the hardest stretch of grief you experience. If the loss was years ago, it may catch you off guard — the season pulling at something you thought had healed. Both are real. Both are valid.

Why grief gets louder in December

The holidays are built out of memory and ritual. The same foods, the same songs, the same gathering patterns — they create a sensory landscape that is closely linked to the people who used to be part of it. When one of those people is gone, the rituals that were once comforting become painful precisely because of how well they remember.

This is not a sign that you're grieving wrong. It's a sign that the relationship mattered.

Memory is stored not just mentally but in the body — in smell, in sound, in the feeling of a particular room at a particular time of year. That's why a song from childhood, a specific candle scent, or the sight of a dish someone used to make can drop you back into grief without warning. Your nervous system isn't broken. It's functioning exactly as it was designed to.

Anticipatory grief: dreading the day before it comes

For many people, the hardest part of a holiday isn't the day itself — it's the weeks leading up to it. You know what's coming. You know the table is going to feel different, the chair is going to be empty, the toast is going to be hard to get through. And so December becomes something to endure rather than something to experience.

This anticipatory grief is real and exhausting. A few things that can help:

  • Name what you're dreading specifically. Vague dread is harder to carry than named grief. Is it the first time around the table? A particular tradition? The drive to a house that used to feel like home? Knowing what you're grieving gives it shape.
  • Give yourself permission to change the rituals. You don't have to do everything the same way it was done when the person was alive. Some families find that keeping the rituals honors the person. Others find that the rituals are too painful and need to be modified or replaced. Both are legitimate.
  • Plan an exit if you need one. Know that it's okay to step outside, to leave early, to need space. You don't have to white-knuckle through an entire gathering.

The pressure to be okay — and the cost of pretending

Grief during the holidays often carries an additional layer: the feeling that you're ruining it for other people. That your sadness is an imposition. That you should be further along by now, that you should be able to hold it together for the kids, for the gathering, for everyone else.

This pressure is worth examining. Some of it is legitimate — you don't have to collapse in grief at every gathering. But some of it is a demand that your pain be invisible for the comfort of people who aren't ready to hold it. That is not a demand you have to meet.

What is true: your grief is not a burden. It is evidence of love. The people who actually care about you don't want you to perform okay when you're not. And the ones who need you to perform okay for their benefit — that is their need, not your obligation.

What actually helps when you're grieving through the holidays

Say the person's name. In many families, there's a collective avoidance of the person who isn't there — as though mentioning them might make it worse. Usually the opposite is true. Saying their name, sharing a memory, laughing at something they used to do — these are acts of love, not invitations to fall apart.

Create a small ritual of remembrance. Light a candle for them. Leave a chair. Cook their recipe. Write them a letter you don't send. These rituals honor the continuing bond and give the grief somewhere to go.

Lower expectations for yourself. You may not feel joy this season, and that's okay. Getting through is enough. Showing up is enough. You don't have to be fully present or fully healed.

Let someone walk with you. Grief isolated is heavier than grief shared. Find at least one person this season who you can be honest with — someone who doesn't need you to be okay, who can sit with you in the weight of it.

What faith holds for the grieving

The Christian calendar was not designed to bypass grief. Advent is a season of longing — of waiting for what has not yet come. The ancient church understood that life involves carrying both hope and sorrow at the same time, that there are things worth weeping over, and that God is not absent from that weeping.

Isaiah 53:3 describes the Messiah as "a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief." This is not incidental. It is theological. The God Christians worship is not distant from human pain — he entered into it. And Revelation 21:4 holds the promise of the end of that grief: "He will wipe every tear from their eyes. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain."

That promise is not yet. But it is coming. And in the meantime, the sorrow is allowed. The tears are held. The absence is real, and it is known.

If you are carrying grief into this season, you don't have to do it alone — not from God, and not from people who can help. That includes therapy, which is one of the most honest places grief is allowed to exist without a performance requirement.
