Men, Stress, and the Holidays: When the Pressure to Hold It Together Becomes Too Much

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you're experiencing significant distress, please reach out to a licensed therapist.

Most conversations about holiday stress are written for women. And while that's not entirely wrong — women do tend to carry more of the visible labor of the season — it misses something important. Men carry their own version of holiday stress, and they carry it quietly.

They carry the financial pressure of a season that costs money. The expectation that they'll show up to gatherings and be present without being emotional. The unspoken role of keeping everyone else okay while managing their own exhaustion. And they do most of this without being asked how they're doing — because nobody asks.

What holiday stress looks like in men

Men under stress often don't look anxious. They look irritable, withdrawn, or checked out. They start picking fights over small things or go emotionally flat. They stay later at work, spend more time in the garage or on the phone, drink a little more than usual. They are present in the room and somewhere else entirely.

These are not character flaws. They are the behavioral expression of emotional overload in a person who was taught — by family, culture, church, locker room — that expressing that overload directly is not an option. So it comes out sideways.

Partners often experience this as coldness or distance. Kids notice Dad isn't really there. The man himself may not be fully aware of what's driving it. He just knows something feels heavy and he doesn't know what to do with that.

The compound pressure men carry

The holidays stack pressures in a way that's specific to men who are providers and protectors:

  • Financial pressure. Gifts, travel, meals, expectations — the season costs money, and the gap between what you want to give and what you can afford is a direct hit to a man's sense of worth as a provider.
  • Family performance pressure. There's often an implicit expectation that he will make the holidays good — that he will manage his extended family, keep the peace, and not need anything himself.
  • End-of-year professional stress. December often brings its own work pressure — deadlines, reviews, year-end closes. That pressure doesn't pause for the holidays.
  • Unresolved relational tension. Holiday gatherings often involve people or conversations that carry weight — estranged family members, old wounds, fathers he doesn't know how to talk to. That weight accumulates.

Why men go quiet when they're struggling

There's a clinical term for what happens to many men under emotional stress: alexithymia. It refers to difficulty identifying and expressing emotions. It doesn't mean men don't have feelings. It means they often haven't been given the language or the safety to work with them.

Many men were raised in environments where emotional expression was modeled poorly or punished outright. Crying made you weak. Saying you were scared or overwhelmed wasn't something men did. The emotional vocabulary simply didn't develop the way it needed to.

So when stress hits, the internal experience is real but the outlet isn't there. The options become: shut down, blow up, or numb out. Many men cycle through all three.

What actually helps

Name it, even roughly. You don't have to have the perfect language. "I'm stressed" or "I'm not okay this week" is a complete sentence. Saying it out loud to your partner, a friend, or a pastor gets it outside your head — and outside is where it can be dealt with.

Identify what specifically is weighing on you. Vague stress is harder to manage than specific stress. Is it money? A particular family member? Exhaustion? Knowing what it is gives you something to actually work with.

Give yourself physical outlets. This is not a platitude. Exercise, manual work, long walks — these are legitimate emotional regulation tools for men. Movement processes stress in the body in a way that ruminating in the same chair cannot.

Have one honest conversation. Not a comprehensive emotional processing session. Just one moment where you say something true to someone who cares about you. That act of connection interrupts isolation before it solidifies.

Lower what you're carrying without lowering who you are. Strength is not the same as self-reliance. The strongest men I've worked with in therapy are the ones who could say "I need help" and mean it. That's not weakness. That's wisdom.

A word to the men reading this

If you're reading this, something in the title connected with something you're carrying. That's not an accident.

You are allowed to be tired. You are allowed to not have it together. You are allowed to need support — not because you've failed, but because you're human. Galatians 6:2 says: "Carry each other's burdens." That's not a command issued only to the people around you. It's permission for you to let them.

Therapy is one of the most underused resources among men, and usually the most useful once they try it. If the weight you're carrying has been there longer than just this season, that's worth taking seriously.
