Christian Men and Depression: Why the Ones Who Need Help Most Are the Hardest to Reach

By Hector L. Figueroa, LMSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you or someone you know is struggling, please reach out to a licensed mental health professional.

There is a specific kind of man I see in my practice. He comes in late, often referred by his wife or his doctor, often after something has already broken down. He is competent, responsible, and deeply committed to his family and his faith. He has been quietly suffering for a long time.

He usually says some version of the same thing in the first session: "I don't know why I'm here. I'm not the kind of person who needs this."

That sentence, more than any diagnostic tool, tells me what I'm working with.

Christian men face a double barrier when it comes to depression. The first is the cultural message that men should be strong, self-sufficient, and in control. The second is the religious message that a man of faith should be able to overcome his struggles through prayer, Scripture, and trust in God. Both of these messages are deeply embedded, and both of them keep men from getting help until things have gotten significantly worse than they needed to.

What Depression Actually Looks Like in Men

Part of the problem is that most people picture depression wrong.

The cultural image of depression is someone who can't get out of bed, crying, withdrawn from the world. That version exists. But in men, especially in men who have been trained to perform competence, depression more often looks like this:

  • Irritability, short fuse, disproportionate anger at small things
  • Increased drinking or other numbing behaviors
  • Burying himself in work, staying late, filling every hour
  • Pulling away from intimacy without being able to explain why
  • Feeling flat rather than sad. Not crying. Just nothing.
  • Losing interest in things he used to love: hobbies, church, his kids
  • Physical symptoms: chronic fatigue, headaches, back pain with no clear cause

When depression looks like irritability and overwork, it doesn't look like depression to the man experiencing it. It looks like stress. It looks like busyness. It looks like being tired because he's working hard. He can explain every symptom away without ever calling it what it is.

And the people around him often can't name it either, because they're too close to it and because they've been taught not to press.

What the Church Teaches Men About Struggle

I want to be careful here, because I am a man of faith and I believe the church does enormous good. But I also have to be honest about what I have seen in two and a half decades of clinical work.

Many Christian men receive, not always explicitly but consistently, a set of messages about what it means to be a man of God:

Be the spiritual leader of your household. Be steady. Be strong. Don't let your family see you waver. Trust God. If you're struggling, it's probably a spiritual issue. Pray through it. Fast through it. Confess it. Lead your family through it.

Those messages are not entirely wrong. There is truth in many of them. But when they become the only framework a man has for understanding his own suffering, they become a trap.

Because when depression shows up, the man who has internalized these messages doesn't say, "I think I need help." He says, "I need to be stronger. I need to pray more. I need to trust God more. My family is depending on me and I can't let them down." And then he white-knuckles it, alone, for months or years, while things get quietly worse.

I have seen this pattern more times than I can count. And what breaks my heart is that the very depth of his faith commitment is part of what keeps him from getting the help that would free him to live out that commitment fully.

The Masculinity Piece

Layer the cultural messages about masculinity on top of the religious ones and you have something very difficult to move.

Men are socialized from childhood to connect their worth to their capacity to handle things. Asking for help is, in the world many of these men grew up in, a form of failure. It means you couldn't handle it. It means you're weak. It means someone else has to carry what you should have been carrying yourself.

For a Christian man, that message gets fused with an additional layer: if I'm struggling this much, there must be something wrong with my faith. The weakness is not just masculine weakness. It is spiritual weakness. And spiritual weakness is something a man is supposed to hide from his family, his church, his pastor, and often from himself.

This is the double bind. The very things that shape him as a man and as a believer are the things making it hardest for him to reach out when he most needs to.

Why the Most Serious Cases Are Often the Last to Seek Help

There is a clinical reality here that I think families need to understand.

Men who are suffering the most are often the best at hiding it. Years of practicing emotional suppression means they have become genuinely good at performing fine when they are not fine. Their wives often know something is wrong but can't name it. Their children sense tension but don't know where it's coming from. Their friends and small group members see a man who shows up, who serves, who leads his family, who appears to have it together.

And internally, he is exhausted. He is running on empty. He feels like a fraud. He is terrified that if people knew what was actually going on inside him, they would think less of him as a man and as a believer.

So he keeps going. And the longer he keeps going, the harder it becomes to stop and say, "I'm not okay."

The threshold for getting help keeps moving. He'll get help when it gets really bad. But really bad is a moving target. He adjusts to each new level of suffering and that becomes the new normal.

What Scripture Actually Has to Say About This

The man Elijah, one of the most powerful prophets in Scripture, reached a point where he sat down under a tree and asked God to let him die. He said, "I have had enough, Lord. Take my life."

God's response was not a rebuke. God did not tell Elijah to be stronger or to trust more. He sent an angel, twice, who touched Elijah gently and said, "Get up and eat. The journey is too much for you."

God met his exhaustion with physical care. With food and rest. With gentleness. Before there was any assignment or vision or calling, there was care for the person who was broken.

David wrote Psalm 88. The whole thing is darkness. It ends in darkness, with no resolution and no turning. It is in the Bible because honest suffering in the presence of God is sacred, not shameful.

Job refused to pretend he was okay. His friends tried to explain away his suffering and God rebuked the friends, not Job.

The biblical model for suffering men is not silence and performance. It is honesty before God and before trustworthy people. The church has sometimes taught the opposite, and it has cost men dearly.

What Actually Breaks Through

In my experience, there are a few things that actually move the Christian man who has been resistant to getting help.

Naming what you see specifically. Not "I think you're depressed." That puts him on the defensive. Instead: "I've noticed you've been irritable every evening for three months. I've noticed you don't enjoy the things you used to enjoy. I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you I'm concerned and I'd like you to talk to someone." Specific observations are harder to dismiss than general worry.

Framing it as stewardship, not weakness. The man who won't go for himself may go for the people who depend on him. "Your family needs you at your best. This is not weakness. This is taking care of what you've been given responsibility for." That frame often resonates where direct appeals to self-care do not.

Finding a therapist who shares his worldview. The fear that a therapist will attack his faith or tell him his beliefs are the problem is real. When he knows he's talking to someone who understands that framework, who won't pathologize his faith, one of the biggest barriers drops. Many of us exist. He should not have to leave his faith at the door of a therapy office.

Letting a trusted man go first. If another man he respects, a pastor, a mentor, a friend, has been in therapy and can say so plainly, that permission is powerful. The stigma holds most of its weight in silence. When one man breaks the silence, it loosens for the men around him.

Not waiting for a crisis. The conversation is much harder after the bottom falls out. If you're reading this because someone you love seems to be struggling, have the conversation now. The man who agrees to go before things fall apart has a different experience than the man who only goes when there's no other option.

A Word to the Man Who Is Reading This

You may have clicked on this because someone sent it to you. You may be reading it and recognizing yourself in some of what I've described. You may be sitting with the familiar feeling that you should be able to handle this on your own.

You are not weak for struggling. You are human.

The men in Scripture who encountered God most deeply were not the ones who had everything together. They were the ones who were honest about what was happening inside them. David. Job. Elijah. Jeremiah. Paul.

Getting help is not a failure of faith. It is an act of honesty. It is choosing to stop performing fine when you are not fine, and that choice takes more courage than the performance does.
