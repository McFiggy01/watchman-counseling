When Your Child Goes to College—and Their Faith Doesn't Go With Them

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you're experiencing significant distress, please reach out to a licensed therapist.

You raised them in church.

Sunday school. Youth group. Vacation Bible School. Christian music in the car. Prayer before meals. Maybe they were baptized, went on mission trips, served in ministry, and could quote Scripture better than some adults.

Then they turned eighteen.

They packed the car. You helped carry boxes into a dorm room. You hugged them goodbye and drove home believing you had prepared them for the next chapter.

And somewhere between eighteen and twenty-one, something changed.

Church became less important.

Prayer became less frequent.

Their beliefs became less certain.

Then one day you hear words you never expected:

"I'm not sure I believe that anymore."

For Christian parents, few sentences hurt more.

But before blaming the university, the professor, their new friends, social media, or "the culture," we need to understand what is actually happening.

The Numbers Are Sobering—but Often Misunderstood

Lifeway Research found that 66% of young adults who attended a Protestant church regularly as teenagers stopped attending regularly for at least one year between ages eighteen and twenty-two.

But notice what that statistic does not say.

It does not say two-thirds became atheists.

It does not say two-thirds rejected Jesus.

It says they stopped attending church regularly.

Those are different things.

And their reasons may surprise you.

Among young adults who stopped attending, Lifeway found the leading individual reasons included:

  • 27% — simply wanted a break from church
  • 26% — believed church members seemed judgmental or hypocritical
  • 25% — moved to college
  • 23% — work responsibilities interfered
  • 22% — moved too far from their church
  • 22% — became too busy
  • 20% — no longer felt connected to people in their church

Life changes were by far the largest overall category of explanations.

That changes the conversation.

The stereotypical story says:

"My Christian kid went to college, met an atheist professor, heard an argument against God and lost his faith."

Certainly, intellectual objections happen.

But frequently something much less dramatic happens first.

They lose their community.

They lose their routine.

They lose the adults who knew their name.

They lose the youth group where they belonged.

And for the first time in their lives, nobody is waking them up Sunday morning saying, "We're going to church."

College may not destroy their faith.

College may simply remove the scaffolding that had been holding it up.

At Eighteen, Borrowed Faith Has to Become Personal Faith

For eighteen years, much of a child's spiritual environment is chosen for them.

Their parents choose the church.

Their parents drive them there.

Their parents determine many of the family's values.

Their parents establish the Sunday routine.

Then adulthood arrives.

Suddenly the young person is asking a different question:

"Do I believe this because I believe it—or because my parents believe it?"

That question is not necessarily rebellion.

It is part of becoming an adult.

There comes a point when the Christianity of Mom and Dad must become their Christianity.

That transition can be uncomfortable because genuine faith has to survive genuine questions.

And sometimes parents become frightened by the questions.

"Do you still believe the Bible?"

"Why aren't you going to church?"

"Who have you been hanging around?"

"What are they teaching you at that school?"

"You know how we raised you."

Behind those questions is usually fear.

But what the young adult often hears is:

"You are disappointing me."

And once that happens, spiritual conversations become debates instead of relationships.

Sometimes It Really Is a Wound

Lifeway found that 26% of church dropouts described church members as judgmental or hypocritical, while another 20% said they did not feel connected to people in their church.

Barna's research likewise points repeatedly toward the importance of relationships in developing resilient faith among young adults.

That matters.

Some young adults aren't walking away because they carefully examined the historical evidence for the resurrection and found it inadequate.

Some are saying:

"I got hurt there."

"I watched Christians preach grace and treat people cruelly."

"I asked questions and was made to feel sinful for asking."

"I never felt like I belonged."

"I watched church leaders behave differently behind closed doors."

Those aren't primarily arguments.

They are experiences.

They are disappointments.

Sometimes they are wounds.

And you cannot debate someone out of a wound.

But Eventually Real Questions About Belief Do Matter

We shouldn't swing too far in the other direction.

Some young adults really do begin questioning Christianity itself.

Recent Pew Research provides an important longer-term picture. Among Americans who have actually left the religion in which they were raised, 46% say stopping belief in its teachings was an extremely or very important reason. Another 38% say religion stopped being important in their lives, while 38% say they gradually drifted away.

So the progression can look something like this:

Disconnection → less participation → less spiritual practice → unresolved questions → changed beliefs.

Not always.

But often enough that parents should pay attention.

The answer, however, is not panic.

And it certainly isn't winning arguments.

Why Arguing Them Back Usually Fails

Imagine your twenty-year-old daughter says:

"I don't know if I believe Christianity is true anymore."

A frightened parent immediately begins presenting evidence.

"But what about the resurrection?"

"Have you read the Bible?"

"Do you realize what Scripture says?"

"Let me send you this video."

"Your professor doesn't know what he's talking about."

There may eventually be a place for apologetics. Christianity makes truth claims, and difficult questions deserve serious answers.

But timing matters.

Psychologists use the term psychological reactance to describe what can happen when people feel that someone is trying to control their beliefs or choices. Forceful persuasive language can actually increase resistance because people instinctively protect their sense of autonomy. Research has found this effect even among college-age populations.

In ordinary language:

The harder you push, the harder they may pull away.

And at eighteen or twenty, independence is one of the developmental tasks they are working hardest to establish.

So don't confuse defending Christianity with defending your anxiety.

Sometimes the most spiritual thing a parent can say is:

"Tell me more. I want to understand."

Questions Are Not the Enemy of Faith

Parents sometimes treat doubt as though it is contagious.

Scripture doesn't.

Thomas questioned.

Job questioned.

Jeremiah questioned.

Habakkuk questioned.

David filled the Psalms with questions.

Even Jesus cried from the cross:

"My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?"

Christianity has survived two thousand years of philosophers, skeptics, empires, scientific discoveries and cultural revolutions.

Your child's sociology professor is not going to destroy Jesus.

So you don't need to be afraid of their questions.

You need to remain close enough that they still feel safe asking them.

Parents: Keep the Bridge Open

If your son or daughter begins distancing themselves from Christianity, resist the temptation to turn every phone call into a sermon.

Don't make church attendance the price of your affection.

Don't interrogate them every time they come home.

Don't send five apologetics videos every time they express doubt.

And don't interpret every question as rebellion.

Instead:

Listen before answering.

Ask, "What changed for you?"

Ask, "Was there something that happened?"

Ask, "What part of Christianity is hardest for you to believe right now?"

Ask, "Have Christians hurt you?"

And perhaps most importantly:

"Do you know that you can tell me what you really think and I will still love you?"

Then listen.

You may discover that the issue isn't evolution.

It isn't sexuality.

It isn't politics.

It isn't even theology.

Your child may simply be lonely.

Or disappointed.

Or hurt.

Or experiencing independence for the first time.

Or trying to determine whether the faith they inherited can become a faith they actually own.

Your Life May Become Your Strongest Argument

Your adult child already knows what you believe.

The bigger question may become:

Does Christianity appear to be producing anything beautiful in you?

Can they disagree with you without losing your affection?

Can they question Christianity without you becoming angry?

Can they watch you apologize?

Can they see humility?

Compassion?

Integrity?

Generosity?

Grace?

Patience?

Jesus said people would recognize His disciples by their love.

Sometimes the strongest apologetic isn't another argument.

It is watching Christ transform a parent.

And Don't Give Up Too Soon

There is another side to the statistics.

Young adults are not necessarily gone forever.

Barna reported in 2025 that younger adults were showing renewed church engagement and spiritual curiosity, with Gen Z and Millennials becoming surprisingly active participants in church attendance compared with older generations. In 2026, many Protestant pastors continued reporting increased Gen Z engagement.

The story is still being written.

The eighteen-year-old questioning Christianity today may be the twenty-eight-year-old passionately following Jesus tomorrow.

Faith journeys rarely move in straight lines.

So parents, pray.

Keep speaking truth.

But speak it with grace.

Keep the door open.

Keep the relationship strong.

Keep demonstrating Christ.

And remember the father in Jesus' story of the prodigal son.

He didn't chase his son into the far country and argue him back home.

He remained the kind of father whose home was still worth returning to.

Maybe that is one of the most important things Christian parents can do when their children begin questioning the faith:

Make sure that while they are figuring out what they believe, they never have to question whether they are still loved.
