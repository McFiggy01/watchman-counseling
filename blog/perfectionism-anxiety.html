Signs You're a Perfectionist (And Why It's Actually an Anxiety Problem)

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you're experiencing significant distress, please reach out to a licensed therapist.

Most people who struggle with perfectionism don't think of it as a problem. They think of it as a personality trait — the thing that makes them thorough, reliable, and high-achieving. Sometimes it is those things. But at a certain point, perfectionism stops being a strength and starts being a source of ongoing distress. And that point is worth paying attention to.

Clinically, perfectionism has more in common with anxiety than it does with conscientiousness. It is driven not by a love of excellence but by a fear of failure, judgment, or loss of worth. That distinction changes everything.

Signs you might be dealing with perfectionism

Perfectionism looks different in different people. Some are visibly high-strung and demanding. Others appear calm while maintaining impossible internal standards. Here are some of the clearest signs:

  • You procrastinate on things that matter most to you. This surprises people, but it makes clinical sense: if you care deeply about something and believe it has to be done perfectly, starting it feels dangerous. What if you try and it isn't good enough?
  • You finish something and immediately see only what's wrong with it. The completed project, the submitted application, the conversation you had — your mind goes straight to the gap between what it was and what it could have been.
  • You over-prepare in ways that eat up significant time and energy. There's a difference between being thorough and spending four hours on something that needed forty-five minutes.
  • Mistakes linger longer than they should. You replay them, re-litigate them, and find it very hard to move on — even from mistakes no one else noticed or cared about.
  • You avoid things you're not already good at. New skills, unfamiliar situations, anything where you might look like a beginner — the discomfort of not being excellent makes it easier to stay away.
  • Your self-worth is tied tightly to your performance. On days when you accomplish a lot and do it well, you feel okay. On days when you struggle or fall short, you feel like a failure as a person — not just someone who had a hard day.

Where perfectionism comes from

Perfectionism is almost always learned, usually early. It develops in environments where love, approval, or safety felt conditional — where being good enough meant performing well, where mistakes had real consequences, where a child had to earn belonging rather than simply receive it.

That child grew into an adult who internalized the message: Your worth depends on your output. The external pressure became an internal one, and now it runs constantly in the background — monitoring, critiquing, pushing.

This is not a character flaw. It's an adaptation. The problem is that the adaptation carries a heavy cost.

The cost nobody talks about

Perfectionism is exhausting. Not occasionally — chronically. When no outcome is ever fully good enough, there is no rest. You achieve something, feel a brief moment of relief, and then the bar moves. The finish line keeps retreating.

Over time, this produces anxiety, burnout, and a persistent low-grade sense that you are always behind, always falling short, always needing to do more. Relationships suffer because other people can never meet the standard either. Creativity shrinks because risk-taking feels too dangerous.

Many perfectionists also struggle with depression — not in spite of their high standards, but because of them. When worth is tied to performance and performance is never quite good enough, the logical endpoint is hopelessness.

What actually shifts this

Separating worth from output. This is the core work, and it's not easy. Worth — your value as a person — cannot be earned or lost through performance. It simply is. That's a theological claim as much as a psychological one: you are made in the image of God and that image is not conditional on your productivity or achievement.

Tolerating "good enough." Deliberately choosing not to revise something for the fourth time. Sending the email when it's clear rather than perfect. Finishing at a reasonable standard and letting that be sufficient. These small acts retrain the nervous system.

Separating mistakes from identity. You made a mistake. That is very different from being a mistake. Practice catching the language your inner critic uses and correcting it toward accuracy: I made an error rather than I am an idiot.

Therapy that addresses the root. Cognitive-behavioral approaches are useful for the surface patterns. But if perfectionism is rooted in conditional worth and early relational experiences, deeper relational work is often what actually moves it. That's what therapy can provide.

Grace as an antidote

There is something the Christian tradition offers the perfectionist that secular self-help often misses: a God whose love is not contingent on performance. The gospel is, at its core, good news for people who know they aren't good enough. Romans 5:8 says: "But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us."

Not when we got it together. Not when we hit the standard. While we were still falling short.

That is not a license to stop caring about excellence. It is freedom to pursue it without your worth on the line. That's a different thing entirely — and for a perfectionist, it can be life-changing.
