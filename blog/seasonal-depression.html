Why You Feel Depressed When the Seasons Change

Understanding seasonal depression, its symptoms, and how to feel like yourself again.

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you are experiencing thoughts of suicide or a mental health crisis, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Have you ever noticed that your mood changes when the seasons do? You feel energetic and motivated in the spring and summer. Then autumn arrives, the days shorten, and something inside you shifts.

You sleep more. You feel exhausted even after a full night's rest. You lose interest in things you normally enjoy, or feel sad without being able to name why. And maybe you catch yourself asking, "What's wrong with me? Why does this happen every year?"

For some people, what they are experiencing is more than the winter blues. It may be Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD), a recognized form of depression that follows a seasonal pattern. The good news: it is real, it is diagnosable, and it responds to treatment.

What is Seasonal Affective Disorder?

Seasonal Affective Disorder is a type of depression that typically begins in the fall or winter and improves as spring arrives. Unlike an occasional bad day, SAD can affect how you think, sleep, eat, and interact with the people around you.

According to the National Institute of Mental Health, SAD involves recurring depressive episodes tied to a seasonal pattern. Most people experience symptoms in the fall and winter, though some experience them in spring and summer.

One important clarification: SAD is not simply disliking cold weather. It involves depressive symptoms that can meaningfully interfere with everyday life.

Why do the changing seasons affect your mental health?

Researchers believe several biological and environmental factors contribute.

Less sunlight disrupts your internal clock. Your body relies on sunlight to regulate its sleep-wake cycle, known as the circadian rhythm. As days grow shorter, reduced daylight can contribute to fatigue, disrupted sleep, and low mood. Getting out of bed may feel harder even after eight or nine hours of sleep.

Brain chemistry may shift with the seasons. Serotonin, a chemical involved in mood regulation, may be affected by changes in sunlight. Melatonin, which governs sleep, can also play a role. These changes may influence your energy, motivation, and emotional state. Read the NIMH overview on SAD

Your daily routine changes. Think about how differently you live in July compared to November. Warmer months often mean more time outside, more social activity, more movement. When darkness arrives earlier and temperatures drop, those routines contract. Less activity and less connection over time can contribute to depressive symptoms.

Seasonal stress makes things worse. For some people, fall and winter bring added emotional weight: grief, loneliness, financial pressure, family conflict, and painful memories tied to the holidays. If you are already managing depression, anxiety, trauma, or relationship strain, seasonal changes can intensify what you are already carrying.

7 signs you may be experiencing seasonal depression

Seasonal depression does not look exactly the same for everyone. Common symptoms include:

  1. Persistent sadness or emptiness that lasts most days, sometimes without an obvious reason
  2. Low energy and fatigue even after adequate sleep
  3. Sleeping more than usual or struggling to get out of bed
  4. Changes in appetite, often involving cravings for carbohydrates or noticeable weight changes
  5. Loss of motivation for tasks or activities you normally handle with ease
  6. Social withdrawal, avoiding people because interacting feels like too much
  7. Difficulty concentrating, mental fog, or trouble staying focused at work

Some people also experience irritability, feelings of hopelessness, or thoughts of death or suicide. Summer-pattern SAD may look different, including insomnia, reduced appetite, agitation, and restlessness.

If these symptoms persist, worsen, or begin interfering with your daily life, speaking with a qualified mental health professional is an important step.

Is it seasonal depression or just the winter blues?

Almost everyone feels the shift when the weather changes. The difference is in how much it costs you.

The winter blues may bring mild sadness, lower motivation, or frustration with shortened days. You can generally keep functioning. Seasonal depression involves more persistent symptoms that interfere with relationships, work, sleep, and daily responsibilities.

Clinicians may consider a seasonal pattern when depressive episodes occur at the same time of year and improve during other seasons, typically over at least two consecutive years. But you do not have to wait two years to ask for help. If you are struggling now, your symptoms deserve attention now.

7 practical ways to manage seasonal depression

You cannot control the weather or the length of the day. But there are real steps you can take.

  1. Get outside during daylight hours. Even a 15-minute walk in the morning gives your body exposure to natural light and encourages movement. You do not need perfect conditions.
  2. Keep a consistent sleep schedule. Depression can disrupt sleep, and irregular patterns make symptoms harder to manage. Try waking and going to bed at the same time each day. Avoid staying in bed when you are awake.
  3. Stay physically active. Exercise supports emotional health, and you do not need a gym or an intense routine. Walking, stretching, dancing, or home workouts all count. Consistency matters more than intensity.
  4. Stay connected to people. When you are depressed, withdrawing feels easier than explaining yourself. But isolation tends to deepen sadness. A phone call, coffee with a friend, or time at a community gathering can help, even when you do not feel like it.
  5. Ask a provider about light therapy. Light therapy is an established treatment for winter-pattern SAD, typically involving a 10,000 lux light box used in the morning. It is not appropriate for everyone. Certain eye conditions, medications, and bipolar disorder require special consideration. Talk with a healthcare professional before starting.
  6. Pay attention to your thoughts. Depression shapes how you interpret your experience. You might notice thoughts like "Nothing ever gets better" or "I am just going to feel this way until spring." These feel convincing when you are in them. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) can help you recognize these patterns and respond more effectively.
  7. Give yourself permission to seek help. You do not have to wait until things are unbearable. Therapy, light therapy, medication, or a combination of approaches can all be effective depending on your symptoms and needs. Reaching out is not weakness. It is a decision to take your mental health seriously.

When seasonal depression points to something deeper

Sometimes the changing seasons do not create emotional struggles as much as they reveal ones that were already there.

During busier months, activity and social life can make it easier to push aside grief, chronic stress, or unresolved pain from the past. When life slows down and you spend more time alone with your thoughts, those emotions become harder to ignore.

This does not mean everyone experiencing SAD has unresolved trauma. It simply means emotional health is shaped by many factors, and the seasons may be one piece of a larger picture. Therapy creates space to explore those experiences without judgment.

You do not have to wait until spring to feel better

One of the most discouraging parts of seasonal depression is believing you have to endure it until the weather changes. But your emotional well-being should not depend on the calendar.

There are effective treatments, supportive relationships, and practical changes that can help you manage symptoms now. And sometimes the most important first step is simply acknowledging that what you are carrying deserves attention.

Frequently asked questions

Can seasonal depression happen even if I enjoy winter?

Yes. SAD is associated with recurring changes in mood and functioning, not with whether someone likes the cold or the season itself.

Can seasonal depression cause anxiety?

Yes. Anxiety symptoms can occur alongside depression. Some people experience increased worry, irritability, or difficulty managing stress during seasonal transitions.

Does vitamin D help with seasonal depression?

Research on vitamin D supplements for SAD has produced mixed findings. If you suspect a deficiency, speak with your healthcare provider about appropriate testing and next steps.

How long does seasonal depression last?

Winter-pattern SAD often begins in the fall or early winter and improves in spring or summer. Duration and severity vary considerably from person to person.

Can therapy help?

Yes. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy adapted for Seasonal Affective Disorder (CBT-SAD) is an evidence-based approach that helps people address unhelpful thinking patterns and maintain meaningful activity during difficult seasons.
