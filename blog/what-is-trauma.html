What Trauma Really Is - And Why It Stays With You

By Hector L. Figueroa, LCSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you are experiencing symptoms of trauma, please reach out to a licensed therapist trained in trauma-focused care.

One of the most common things I hear from people who've been through something hard is a version of this: "I know it was a long time ago. I know I should be over it by now. I don't understand why it still affects me the way it does."

That confusion is part of what makes trauma so isolating. The event is in the past. Life has moved on. And yet something inside hasn't. Understanding why that happens - and what trauma actually is - is often the first step toward healing.

Trauma Is Not What Happened. It's What Happened Inside You.

This is one of the most important distinctions in trauma work, and Dr. Gabor Maté puts it plainly: trauma is not the event itself. Trauma is the wound the event left behind - the way your nervous system, your beliefs about yourself, and your capacity for connection were altered by what you experienced.

That's why two people can go through the same event and have very different outcomes. It isn't about strength or weakness. It's about what was already in place - your age at the time, the relationships around you, your previous experiences, the meaning you made of what happened - and whether the support existed to help your system process and integrate it.

When it doesn't get integrated, it stays. Not as a memory you think about, necessarily - but as something your body and nervous system carry, often without you being fully aware of it.

Big T and Small T: Both Count

People often disqualify their own pain because they don't think what they went through is "bad enough" to call trauma. They compare their experience to something more severe and conclude they don't have the right to struggle.

Clinically, we distinguish between what's sometimes called "Big T" and "small t" trauma. Big T trauma includes things like combat, sexual assault, serious accidents, natural disasters, and life-threatening events. These are the experiences most people associate with PTSD.

But small t traumas - chronic emotional neglect, persistent criticism from a parent, humiliation in childhood, a painful divorce, the slow erosion of a relationship - can be just as shaping. Sometimes more so, because they happen repeatedly and during formative periods, and because they're harder to name and validate.

If something hurt you in a lasting way, that matters. The severity of what happened does not determine whether you deserve to heal.

Why the Past Keeps Showing Up in the Present

Trauma stays because of how the brain processes - or fails to process - overwhelming experience. Under normal conditions, the brain takes in an event, moves it through memory consolidation, and files it as the past. You can recall it, but it feels like something that happened, not something happening now.

Under overwhelming stress, that process breaks down. The event gets stored in a fragmented, sensory-laden way - not as a narrative memory, but as physical sensation, emotional flooding, images, sounds, or the kind of dread that shows up without a clear reason. The nervous system stays partly activated, as though the threat is still present.

This is why trauma can be triggered by things that seem unrelated to the original event - a tone of voice, a smell, a time of year, a certain kind of silence. These are not signs of weakness or irrationality. They are signs that the nervous system is doing its job: warning you of something it once learned was dangerous.

Signs You May Be Carrying Unprocessed Trauma

  • Difficulty trusting people, especially in close relationships
  • Emotional reactions that feel disproportionate to what's actually happening
  • Periods of emotional numbness or disconnection from yourself
  • Hypervigilance - always scanning for what might go wrong
  • Trouble sleeping, intrusive thoughts, or vivid nightmares
  • A persistent sense of shame or the feeling that something is fundamentally wrong with you
  • Physical symptoms - chronic tension, fatigue, stomach issues - with no clear medical cause

Healing Is Possible - But It Requires the Right Kind of Work

Talking about trauma isn't always enough to heal it. That's not a criticism of talk therapy - it's a recognition that trauma lives in the body and the nervous system, not just in conscious thought. Effective trauma treatment often combines cognitive work with approaches that address the physiological dimensions of what you're carrying.

Evidence-based treatments like EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing), trauma-focused CBT, and somatic approaches have strong research support. What they share is a focus on helping the nervous system complete what it couldn't finish at the time - moving the experience from something that feels present into something that is genuinely past.

What Faith Offers in the Middle of Trauma

Psalm 34:18 says: "The Lord is close to the brokenhearted and saves those who are crushed in spirit." That verse isn't a promise that pain won't come. It's a promise about where God is when it does.

In my work, I've seen that for many people, their faith becomes both a resource and a site of wounding - sometimes the same wound. Trauma can shake our theology, challenge our image of God, and make prayer feel impossible. Part of the work, sometimes, is tending to that too.

Healing from trauma is not just psychological. It's often spiritual - a slow reclaiming of safety, of trust, of the belief that you are known and that you matter. That work is worth doing.

You Don't Have to Keep Carrying This

If what you've read here resonates - if something in you recognizes the pattern - it may be time to work with someone who understands trauma and how to treat it. You don't have to have the words for it yet. You just have to be willing to begin.
