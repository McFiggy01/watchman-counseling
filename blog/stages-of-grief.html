The Stages of Grief: Learning to Live With What You've Lost

By Hector L. Figueroa, LMSW

Note: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. If you are struggling with grief and need support, please consider reaching out to a licensed therapist.

Grief is one of the few experiences almost everyone will face, yet nobody experiences it exactly the same way.

We often associate grief with the death of someone we love, but grief can follow many kinds of loss: divorce, the end of a friendship, retirement, losing a job, receiving a serious diagnosis, watching children leave home, or even realizing that the future you imagined is no longer going to happen.

Sometimes grief is not simply missing a person.

It is grieving the life you thought you were going to have.

Grief Does Not Follow a Straight Line

Many people have heard about the five stages of grief: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance.

Psychiatrist Elisabeth Kubler-Ross introduced this framework in 1969. Over time, it became widely used to help describe emotional responses associated with loss.

But there is an important misunderstanding.

Grief is not a five-step program.

You do not finish denial, graduate to anger, move through depression, and eventually arrive at acceptance. The American Psychological Association notes that these experiences may overlap, repeat, or happen in a completely different order.

You might feel peaceful Monday, furious Tuesday, completely fine Wednesday, and overwhelmed by sadness after hearing a song Thursday morning.

That does not mean you are going backward. It means you are grieving.

Stage 1: Denial

This Can't Be Happening

Denial does not always mean literally refusing to believe what happened.

Sometimes it looks like emotional numbness. You may go to work, answer emails, take care of the children, attend the funeral, handle paperwork, and appear remarkably calm. Then weeks later, the reality begins to sink in.

Denial can temporarily protect us from absorbing more emotional pain than we are prepared to handle at once. There is no need to force yourself to feel everything immediately. Sometimes the mind allows reality to enter slowly.

Stage 2: Anger

Why Did This Happen?

Grief can make people angry. Angry at doctors. Angry at family. Angry at themselves. Angry at the person who died. Angry at life. And for people with spiritual beliefs, sometimes even angry at God.

People occasionally feel guilty about that anger, believing they should be stronger, more grateful, or more spiritual. But anger often has pain underneath it.

"What were you thinking?" may really mean, "I miss you."

"This isn't fair" may really mean, "I wasn't ready to lose you."

Healthy grieving allows room for those emotions without allowing them to control every decision we make.

Stage 3: Bargaining

If Only...

Bargaining often lives in the world of if only.

"If only I had called sooner." "If only we had gone to another doctor." "If only I had noticed something was wrong." "If only I hadn't said that."

Sometimes people mentally replay events hundreds of times trying to create a different ending. Bargaining is understandable because loss often makes us feel powerless. Our minds search for a moment where perhaps we could have changed what happened.

But there comes a point when "if only" becomes emotionally exhausting. A helpful question to sit with is this one:

"Am I remembering the past, or am I putting myself on trial for it?"

There is a difference.

Stage 4: Depression

I Feel the Weight of This

As the reality of loss settles in, profound sadness may follow. Energy may decrease. Motivation may disappear. Sleep and appetite may change. Ordinary activities may feel meaningless for a while.

You may withdraw socially because explaining how you feel requires more energy than you have.

Sadness during grief is not automatically a mental health disorder. Grief is a natural response to loss, and there is no universal timetable for how long it lasts.

However, when grief remains extremely intense, significantly interferes with everyday functioning, or leaves someone feeling unable to move forward for an extended period, professional support can help. Prolonged Grief Disorder is now recognized in the DSM-5-TR and involves persistent, severe grief that substantially interferes with daily life.

Seeking counseling does not mean you have failed at grieving. Sometimes you simply need someone to help you carry what has become too heavy to carry alone.

Stage 5: Acceptance

This Happened, and I Am Learning to Live With It

Acceptance may be the most misunderstood stage.

Acceptance does not mean: "I'm happy this happened." It does not mean "I don't miss them anymore" or "It doesn't hurt" or "I've moved on."

Acceptance sounds more like this:

"I wish this had never happened, but I recognize that it did, and I am learning how to live my life from here."

You can accept reality and still cry. You can laugh again and still miss someone. You can build a new life without betraying the life you had before.

Healing does not require forgetting.

Where Spirituality Can Help

For many people, grief raises spiritual questions that ordinary conversation cannot answer.

Why did this happen? Where is God in suffering? Will I ever see this person again? What does my life mean now?

Spirituality can offer something grief desperately needs: meaning, connection, hope, and the belief that our suffering does not have the final word. That does not mean covering pain with religious phrases.

Sometimes the most spiritual thing a grieving person can do is simply say: "I'm hurting."

The Bible itself contains people grieving honestly, questioning God, crying, lamenting, and trying to understand suffering. Faith does not always remove grief. Sometimes faith simply gives us somewhere to bring it.

There Is No Deadline for Missing Someone

One of the most harmful messages grieving people hear is: "You should be over this by now."

There is no universal date when someone should stop grieving. Birthdays may hurt. Holidays may feel different. A particular restaurant, smell, photograph, song, or anniversary may bring memories rushing back years later.

Healing is not measured by whether you ever feel sadness again. A better measure is whether grief gradually becomes something you can carry while continuing to participate in life.

At first, grief can feel like the entire story. Eventually, it can become one chapter of a much larger story.

Give Yourself Permission to Grieve

You do not have to grieve perfectly. You do not have to be strong every day. You do not have to explain every emotion.

Talk with people you trust. Maintain basic routines when possible. Eat. Sleep. Move your body. Pray or reflect if spirituality is meaningful to you. Allow yourself to remember. Allow yourself to laugh without guilt.

And when the weight becomes too much, reach out. Sometimes healing begins with a very simple sentence:

"I think I need someone to talk to."

Grief changes us. But change does not mean that life is over.

With time, support, compassion, and sometimes faith, we can learn to carry what we have lost while slowly rediscovering what remains.

And perhaps that is one of the deepest truths about grief: moving forward does not mean leaving someone, or something you loved, behind.
